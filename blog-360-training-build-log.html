Build log: what we learned making a 360° training prototype

By the JJKS team9 October 20265 min read

Our first client project was an interactive 360° health and safety training platform for a logistics and warehousing company in the automotive sector. Here's an honest look at how it went and what we'd tell anyone starting a similar project.

Starting with the brief, out loud

In our first meeting we didn't open with ideas. We read our understanding of the brief back to the client: who they are, what wasn't working, what they'd asked for. It felt slightly awkward. It was also the most useful ten minutes of the project, because it gave the client an easy moment to correct us before we'd built anything.

Writing the specification early

Within days we had a first draft of the Software Requirements Specification. It listed the users, the features and, just as importantly, what was out of scope for this phase. The client reviewed it in our second meeting, asked for a change (VR headsets would be optional), and we updated it the same week.

From then on, every conversation could point back to an agreed document. That made progress reviews short and focused.

Depth over breadth

The brief mentioned six training topics, from manual handling to cyber awareness. With four of us and a fixed timeline, building all six properly wasn't realistic. We said so early and focused on doing the core experience well: a 360° scene where trainees explore, spot hazards, make a decision and get a score.

Knowing what we don't know

We're software developers, not safety consultants. So the specification made it clear that the client's own experts check every piece of safety content for accuracy, and our tester checked the software. Being clear about who owns what built trust rather than undermining it.

What we'd do again

  • Play the brief back in the very first meeting.
  • Get the specification confirmed in writing before building.
  • Send short minutes after every meeting, with named owners and deadlines for each action.
  • Be upfront about scope. Clients appreciate honesty far more than over-promising.
